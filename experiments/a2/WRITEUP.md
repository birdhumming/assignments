## How to read the numbers

All language-model numbers are **final validation loss** (nats) for the d8 baseline model
(8 layers, width 512, 1% warmup then linear decay, batch 64, AdamW, weight decay 0.1) unless a
row says otherwise. From Assignment 1 we know the seed-to-seed standard deviation of this model is
about **0.002**, so:

- two runs within 0.005 are the same run;
- a gap of 0.01 is real but small;
- a gap of 0.1 is a different regime.

A second scale matters in this assignment: how flat the loss is around the optimal learning rate.
For every budget we fitted, moving the learning rate by a factor of 2 from the optimum costs only
0.001–0.01. That flatness is why many "predictions" below land within noise of the best run even
when the predicted learning rate is 40% off. It is also why fitted optima have wide error bars.

## The five things to remember

1. **Optimal learning rate barely moves with training budget for AdamW**, and the little movement
   it has is not a clean power law (R² 0.41 over six budgets). You get almost all of the gain by
   picking a learning rate in the flat region (0.002–0.004) and spending the compute elsewhere.
2. **Learning rate and weight decay are coupled through their product.** The product
   `lr × wd` follows a tight power law in tokens (R² 0.97) while each coordinate alone does not;
   fixing the learning rate and setting weight decay from the product rule recovered the tuned loss
   at 2.5B tokens to within 0.001.
3. **Batch-size rules depend on the noise regime.** In the toy quadratic model the optimal
   learning rate grows almost linearly with batch when gradient noise is small (exponent 0.9–1.0),
   but only like the square root when noise dominates (exponent 0.45–0.55). Momentum helps at
   small batch and hurts at large batch.
4. **µP is about keeping the first few updates width-independent.** The derivation says: hidden
   learning rate ∝ 1/width, readout multiplier ∝ 1/width, embedding untouched. Kaiming's tuned
   learning rate falls roughly like 1/width; µP's stays put.
5. **Fits extrapolate worse than they interpolate.** The three-budget AdamW fit over-predicted
   the next budgets; the all-six-budget fit was noisier than the three-large-budget fit; the
   Hyperball fit from three small budgets landed inside the target's flat region but 20% below
   the measured optimum. Always report the loss gap, not just the predicted hyperparameter.

## Problem 1 — When can training on less data guide large-scale runs?

**Question.** If you tune the learning rate on small token budgets, can you predict the right
learning rate for a bigger budget? Does the trend continue, and does a fancier optimizer
(Hyperball) scale more predictably?

### (a) Small budgets (supplied runs)

Local quadratic fit in log learning rate at each budget, three supplied points per budget.

| tokens | lr 0.0015 | lr 0.003 | lr 0.006 | fitted lr* | fitted loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 153.6M | 3.2442 | 3.2291 | 3.3056 | 0.00238 | 3.2240 |
| 307.2M | 3.0709 | 3.0562 | 3.0715 | 0.00298 | 3.0562 |
| 614.4M | 2.9473 | 2.9256 | 2.9408 | 0.00319 | 2.9255 |

Power law through those three optima: `lr* ∝ tokens^+0.21` (R² 0.91). The optimum drifts slowly
*upward* with budget on this range.

### (b) Predict, then look (supplied runs)

Predictions recorded from the small-budget rule before looking at the larger budgets:

| tokens | predicted lr* | fitted lr* after looking | fitted loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.2288B | 0.00380 | 0.00388 | 2.8368 |
| 1.8432B | 0.00414 | 0.00343 | 2.7964 |
| 2.4576B | 0.00440 | 0.00291 | 2.7682 |

The trend does **not** continue. The optimum peaks near 1.2B tokens and then drifts back down;
the small-budget rule over-predicts by 20–50% at 1.8B and 2.5B. In loss terms it does not matter
much: lr 0.003 is the best sampled point at every budget, and the curvature keeps shrinking
(0.095 → 0.014), so the penalty for being 40% off at 2.5B is about 0.001.

### (c) Two rules, tested at 4.9152B tokens (5 new runs, ~74 min each)

Fits recorded before launching:

| rule | law | R² | predicted lr* at 4.9B |
|---|---|---|---|
| all six budgets | lr* ∝ tokens^+0.10 | 0.41 | 0.00373 |
| three largest budgets | lr* ∝ tokens^−0.41 | 0.97 | 0.00223 |

Prediction before running: the three-largest fit should win, because the small budgets are in a
different regime (the optimum is still rising there) and pollute the trend you actually want to
extrapolate. Measured:

| lr | val loss | gap to best sampled |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0015 | 2.7176 | +0.0007 |
| **0.00223** (larger-three) | **2.7169** | 0 |
| 0.003 (transfer 2.5B best) | 2.7176 | +0.0007 |
| 0.00373 (all-six) | 2.7185 | +0.0017 |
| 0.006 | 2.7273 | +0.0105 |

Fitted target optimum 0.00214 (loss 2.7169). The larger-three rule hit it; the all-six rule was
off by 1.7× and cost 0.0017; simply reusing 0.003 cost 0.0007.

**Answer.** Yes, small-budget tuning tells you where the flat region is, and the three-large-budget
fit predicted the 4.9B optimum essentially exactly. But the *gain* from fitting versus just
carrying over the previous best learning rate is 0.0007 — a third of the seed noise. The fits buy
you confidence that nothing is about to go wrong, not a lower loss.

**How to think about it.**
1. Separate "where is the optimum" from "how much does it cost to miss it". Here the curvature
   at 4.9B is tiny, so the second question dominates and every reasonable choice is fine.
2. Bias–variance: six points give lower variance but include budgets from a different regime
   (bias). Three points have higher variance but are the right regime. With a monotone trend and
   a decade of extrapolation, picking the right regime mattered more than the extra points.
3. A low R² (0.41) is the fit telling you it is not a power law. Do not extrapolate it.
4. Extrapolate at most one doubling with confidence. From 2.5B to 4.9B is one doubling and the
   three-point fit was exact; the small-three rule extrapolated two doublings and was 50% off.

### (d) Hyperball versus AdamW (4 new runs at 1.2288B tokens, ~24 min each)

Hyperball = Adam direction, but each weight matrix is rescaled after every step to keep its
initial Frobenius norm, so there is no weight decay. Supplied sweeps (8 learning rates at each of
153.6M, 307.2M, 614.4M tokens):

| tokens | Hyperball fitted lr* | Hyperball fitted loss | AdamW fitted lr* | AdamW fitted loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 153.6M | 0.0156 | 3.2009 | 0.00238 | 3.2240 |
| 307.2M | 0.0122 | 3.0319 | 0.00298 | 3.0562 |
| 614.4M | 0.0104 | 2.9176 | 0.00319 | 2.9255 |

Power laws: Hyperball `lr* ∝ tokens^−0.29` (R² 0.985); AdamW `lr* ∝ tokens^+0.21` (R² 0.91).
Prediction recorded before running: Hyperball lr* at 1.2288B = **0.00839**.

| lr | val loss |
|---|---|
| 0.006 | 2.8437 |
| 0.00839 (predicted) | 2.8291 |
| **0.01** | **2.8271** |
| 0.015 | 2.8358 |

Fitted target optimum 0.0102, loss 2.8270. The prediction was 18% low and cost 0.002 — one seed
standard deviation. Hyperball's best (2.8271) also beats AdamW's best at the same budget (2.8378)
by 0.011.

**Answer.** Hyperball's optimal learning rate does vary more regularly: a clean monotone decrease
with a tight fit (R² 0.985) versus AdamW's non-monotone wobble. The three-budget extrapolation
still under-shot the optimum by about 20%, because three points spanning a factor of 4 give you a
slope with maybe ±0.1 uncertainty in the exponent, and one more doubling turns that into ±10–20%
in the prediction. Regular is not the same as precise.

**How to think about it.**
1. Why Hyperball is more regular: the weight norm is pinned, so the effective step size is just
   `lr × ‖W‖/‖U‖` — one knob. AdamW's effective step depends on the weight norm, which weight
   decay and the learning rate change together over training. Fewer moving parts, cleaner trend.
2. Why its optimum falls with budget while AdamW's rises then falls: with a fixed norm, longer
   training means more steps that each need to be smaller to settle. AdamW at small budgets is
   also fighting under-training, which pushes its optimum up first.
3. Three budgets is the minimum for a power law and gives no estimate of its own error. Treat
   any three-point extrapolation as "within a factor of 1.5".

## Problem 2 — How should learning rate and weight decay scale together?

**Question.** When both the learning rate and weight decay are tuned, do their optima move with
budget separately or together? Can a fixed learning rate be rescued by setting weight decay
correctly?

### (a) Joint fits (supplied 3×3 grids)

Local quadratic in (log lr, log wd) per budget; all four fits are proper minima.

| tokens | fitted lr* | fitted wd* | lr*·wd* | fitted loss | best sampled (lr, wd) → loss | P1 best at same budget | gain from tuning wd |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 153.6M | 0.00186 | 0.847 | 1.57e-3 | 3.1766 | (0.0015, 1.6) → 3.1844 | 3.2291 | 0.045 |
| 307.2M | 0.00160 | 0.519 | 8.33e-4 | 3.0309 | (0.0015, 0.8) → 3.0282 | 3.0562 | 0.028 |
| 614.4M | 0.00247 | 0.258 | 6.37e-4 | 2.9175 | (0.003, 0.2) → 2.9178 | 2.9256 | 0.008 |
| 1.2288B | 0.00270 | 0.162 | 4.37e-4 | 2.8349 | (0.003, 0.2) → 2.8361 | 2.8378 | 0.002 |

Trends: the optimal learning rate wanders (0.0019 → 0.0016 → 0.0025 → 0.0027) just like in
Problem 1, while the optimal weight decay falls steadily (0.85 → 0.16, a factor of 5 over 8× the
tokens). The benefit of tuning weight decay shrinks from 0.045 at the smallest budget to 0.002
(noise) at 1.2B.

### (b) Product law

| quantity | power law in tokens | R² |
|---|---|---|
| lr* | ∝ tokens^+0.22 | 0.68 |
| wd* | ∝ tokens^−0.82 | 0.994 |
| lr*·wd* | ∝ tokens^−0.59 | 0.967 |

On every contour plot the long axis of the ellipse runs along the line `lr × wd = constant`:
raising one and lowering the other by the same factor barely changes the loss, while moving along
the product direction changes it a lot. Weight decay alone has the tightest law here, but that is
partly because the learning rate was nearly constant across these budgets; the product is the
quantity with a physical meaning (see below), and it is nearly as tight.

### (c) Test at 2.4576B tokens (5 new runs, ~38 min each)

Product rule fitted on the four source budgets predicts `lr*·wd* = 2.78e-4` at 2.4576B, so at
peak learning rate 0.003 the predicted weight decay is **0.0927**.

| lr | wd | lr·wd | val loss | gap to prediction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0015 | 1.6 (baseline i: best 153.6M pair, untuned) | 2.4e-3 | 2.8517 | +0.084 |
| 0.003 | 0.05 | 1.5e-4 | 2.7715 | +0.004 |
| **0.003** | **0.0927 (prediction)** | 2.8e-4 | **2.7680** | — |
| 0.003 | 0.1 (baseline ii grid best) | 3.0e-4 | 2.7671 | −0.001 |
| 0.003 | 0.2 | 6.0e-4 | 2.7725 | +0.005 |

Quadratic in log wd at lr 0.003: wd* = 0.096, loss 2.7675.

**Answer.** Yes, the product rule gives an effective recipe. The predicted weight decay lands
within noise of the tuned grid best (gap −0.001, i.e. the grid was marginally better) and 0.084
better than carrying over the small-budget pair. The large loss from baseline (i) is the real
lesson: a weight decay of 1.6 that was optimal at 153.6M tokens is 17× too large at 2.5B.

**How to think about it.**
1. Why the product: with AdamW the weights decay by a factor `(1 − lr·wd)` per step, so
   `1/(lr·wd)` is the memory length in steps — how many recent updates the weights "remember".
   Two recipes with the same product forget at the same rate; that is why the contours are
   diagonal.
2. Why the product falls with budget: longer training needs a longer memory relative to a step
   but not relative to the whole run. Empirically `lr·wd ∝ tokens^−0.6`, so the memory length in
   *steps* grows like tokens^0.6 — a bit slower than the run length itself.
3. Why the weight-decay benefit shrinks with budget: at small budgets the model is under-trained
   and a large decay keeps the weights small enough that the still-large learning rate is
   effective; by 1.2B tokens the default `wd = 0.1` is already near the optimum.
4. Fixing one coordinate and solving for the other works because the loss valley is long and
   narrow along the product direction. Hold the learning rate anywhere in its flat region and set
   `wd = predicted product / lr`.

## Problem 3.1 — What does the noisy quadratic model predict about batch size? (CPU)

**Question.** In a 2-D quadratic with noisy gradients and a fixed dataset of N = 8192 samples
(so bigger batches mean fewer steps), how should the optimal learning rate scale with batch size,
does it depend on the optimizer, on the noise level, and does momentum help?

Setup: `f(w) = ½ wᵀ H w`, H = diag(1, 10), gradient noise covariance σ²I, w₀ ~ N(0, 1) ⊕
N(0, 0.1), 1024 samples per estimate. Source batches 1–64, fit `lr* ∝ B^p`, predict B = 256,
test with a sweep at 256.

### (a)–(b) Scaling exponent by optimizer (σ = 1)

| optimizer | exponent p (B 1–64) | predicted lr*(256) | measured lr*(256) | loss at predicted | tuned loss | gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SGD | 0.99 | 0.184 | 0.123 | 0.00237 | 0.00073 | +0.0016 |
| RMSProp | 0.90 | 0.113 | 0.094 | 0.0151 | 0.0121 | +0.0031 |
| Adam | 0.92 | 0.125 | 0.156 | 0.0149 | 0.0144 | +0.0005 |

All three scale almost linearly with batch in the low-noise regime. The linear rule for SGD
breaks exactly where you expect: at B = 256 the predicted learning rate 0.184 is at the stability
edge for the stiff direction (`lr < 2/10 = 0.2`), so the measured optimum backs off to 0.123.
Adam and RMSProp have similar exponents but Adam transfers better (gap 0.0005 vs 0.0031): the
same exponent does not imply the same transfer accuracy because the loss-vs-lr curves have
different widths.

Tuned SGD loss by batch: 0.00037 (B 1) → 0.00032 (B 8–16) → 0.00039 (B 64) → 0.00073 (B 256).
The sweet spot is B ≈ 8–16; past that the best tuned loss rises, because at fixed N the number of
updates (N/B) is what you are paying with, and by B = 256 there are only 32 of them. The rule "lr ∝ B" holds while noise dominates the
error (small B); once the budget is only a handful of steps the stability ceiling and the finite
step count take over.

**Prediction for the language model:** optimal learning rate rises with batch size, close to
linearly from batch 8 to 64, flattening by 128–256 where the per-step noise is no longer the
bottleneck. (Tested in P3.2.)

### (c) Noise level

| σ | RMSProp exponent | Adam exponent |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.91 | 0.92 |
| 10 | 0.55 | 0.54 |
| 100 | 0.45 | 0.49 |
| 300 | 0.53 | 0.49 |

Scalar curvature model (`f = w²/2`): SGD 0.99; RMSProp 0.94 → 0.57 → 0.50 → 0.40; Adam 0.93 →
0.55 → 0.48 → 0.45 as σ goes 1 → 300.

**Answer.** Yes, the signal-to-noise ratio changes the rule. With adaptive optimizers the second
moment `v ≈ g² + σ²/B`: when the gradient dominates, `v` is batch-independent and the update
`g/√v` behaves like SGD, so the optimal lr ∝ B. When noise dominates, `√v ∝ σ/√B` already scales
the update by √B, and the optimal learning rate only needs to supply another √B — exponent ½. The
result is the same for the 2-D and scalar models, so it is a property of the second-moment
normalisation, not of the curvature. What *is* curvature-sensitive is where the linear rule
breaks: in 2-D the stiff direction imposes `lr < 2/λ_max`, which the scalar model lacks.

### (d) Momentum at small and large batch

| setting | B = 16: lr* → loss | B = 256: lr* → loss |
|---|---|---|
| SGD, µ = 0 | 0.0126 → 0.00035 | 0.124 → 0.00067 |
| SGD, µ = 0.9 | 0.00127 → 0.00032 | 0.0083 → 0.0041 |
| Adam, β₁ = 0 | 0.0089 → 0.00104 | 0.093 → 0.0123 |
| Adam, β₁ = 0.5 | 0.0089 → 0.00082 | 0.133 → 0.0035 |
| Adam, β₁ = 0.9 | 0.0090 → 0.00083 | 0.156 → 0.0143 |
| Adam, β₁ = 0.95 | 0.0090 → 0.00094 | 0.188 → 0.050 |
| Adam, β₁ = 0.99 | 0.018 → 0.0127 | 0.064 → 0.134 |

**Answer.** Momentum is mildly helpful at small batch (SGD 0.00035 → 0.00032; Adam β₁ 0.5–0.9
beat β₁ = 0 by 20%) and clearly harmful at large batch when the learning rate is retuned
(SGD µ = 0.9 is 6× worse; Adam β₁ ≥ 0.9 is worse than β₁ = 0.5). With 32 updates at B = 256,
momentum with β₁ = 0.9 spends 10 of them just winding up, and the stiff direction overshoots.

**How to think about it.**
1. Momentum averages gradients over ~1/(1−β₁) steps. That is noise reduction, which is exactly
   what a bigger batch already does — so the benefit shrinks as batch grows.
2. The cost is lag: at large batch and few steps, the averaging window is a big fraction of the
   run and the effective learning rate `lr/(1−β₁)` must be cut 10×, which is what the tuned SGD
   lr* shows (0.124 → 0.0083).
3. Expectation for the language model: β₁ = 0.9 will matter little or help slightly at batch 8,
   and matter little at batch 256 because the LM run has thousands of steps, not 32. (Tested in
   P3.2(c).)

## Problem 4.0 — Scaling rules from alignment assumptions

Full derivation with all four cases in `P40_derivation.md`. The short version:

| assumption (update alignment α, readout alignment ω) | hidden lr | readout multiplier | readout init variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| aligned, aligned (1, 1) — **µP** | η/m | 1/m | 1/n₀ |
| aligned, not (1, ½) | η/m | 1/m | grows with width — inconsistent |
| not aligned, aligned (½, 1) | η/√m | 1/√m | 1/n |
| neither (½, ½) | η/√m | 1/√m | 1/n₀ |

Hidden init variance is 1/fan-in and embedding (variance 1, lr η) in every row. Adam's update is
sign-like and strongly correlated with its input, so α = 1 is the working assumption, and
consistency then forces ω = 1 — the µP row. Kaiming (hidden lr η, readout multiplier 1, readout
variance 1/n) is the "neither" row's initialisation with the "aligned" row's update: its first
step moves features by Θ(width), so its tuned base learning rate must fall like 1/width.
