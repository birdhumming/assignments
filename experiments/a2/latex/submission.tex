Two things follow. First, small budget tuning does tell you where the flat region is, and a fit over the right regime (the three large budgets, extrapolated by one doubling) predicted the 4.9B optimum almost exactly. On the choice of points: using all six lowers the variance of the fit but adds bias from a regime where the optimum was still rising. When the trend changes direction, choosing the right regime matters more than having more points, and a low $R^2$ is the fit telling you not to extrapolate it. One doubling of extrapolation was safe; the small three rule extrapolated two doublings and was 50\% off.

\subsection*{(d) Hyperball versus AdamW (4 new runs at 1.2288B tokens)}

The fitted optimum is 0.0102 (loss 2.8270). The prediction was 18\% low and cost 0.002, one seed standard deviation. Hyperball's best loss (2.8271) also beats AdamW's best at this budget (2.8378) by 0.011.

Hyperball's optimum moves more predictably than AdamW's: a clean, monotone decrease with a tight power law instead of a wobble. The reason is mechanical. With each weight matrix pinned to its initial norm, the effective step is $\eta\lVert W\rVert/\lVert U\rVert$ and the learning rate is the only knob. In AdamW the effective step depends on a weight norm that the learning rate and weight decay move together during training.

Predictable is not the same as precise. Three source budgets spanning $4\times$ pin the exponent to roughly $\pm0.1$, and one more doubling turns that into a 10 to 20\% error in the predicted learning rate, which is what we saw. Three points give no estimate of their own error, so a three point extrapolation should be read as ``within a factor of 1.5'' and swept around.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{p2_trends.png}
\caption{Left: fitted $\lrs$, $\wds$, and their product vs.\ tokens with power laws; the P2(c) prediction ($\times$) and the measured optimum at 2.4576B ($\square$). Right: best measured loss with WD fixed at 0.1 (P1) vs.\ jointly tuned (P2), and the difference.}
\label{fig:p2t}
\end{figure}

One hyperparameter does compensate for the other. On every contour plot the valley runs along $\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd}=\text{const}$: raising one and lowering the other by the same factor barely changes the loss, while moving across that line changes it a lot. The product follows a tight power law ($R^2=0.97$). Weight decay alone fits even better here (0.994), but only because the learning rate was nearly constant across these budgets. The product is the quantity with a physical meaning. AdamW shrinks the weights by a factor $(1-\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd})$ every step, so $1/(\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd})$ is the number of updates the weights remember.

\subsection*{(c) Test of the joint rule at 2.4576B tokens (5 new runs)}

The product law fitted to the four source budgets predicts $\lrs\cdot\wds=2.78\times10^{-4}$ at 2.4576B tokens. At the peak learning rate 0.003 that gives a \textbf{predicted weight decay of 0.0927}.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{rlrrr}\toprule
lr & wd & lr$\cdot$wd & val loss & gap to prediction\\\midrule
0.0015 & 1.6 --- baseline (i): best 153.6M pair, untuned & $2.4\times10^{-3}$ & 2.8517 & $+0.084$\\
0.003 & 0.05 & $1.5\times10^{-4}$ & 2.7715 & $+0.004$\\
\textbf{0.003} & \textbf{0.0927 (prediction)} & $2.8\times10^{-4}$ & \textbf{2.7680} & ---\\
0.003 & 0.1 --- baseline (ii): grid best & $3.0\times10^{-4}$ & 2.7671 & $-0.001$\\

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A quadratic in $\log\mathrm{wd}$ at lr 0.003 puts the optimum at 0.096 with loss 2.7675. The prediction is within noise of the tuned grid best ($-0.001$) and 0.084 better than carrying over the small budget pair.

The product rule works as a recipe: hold the learning rate anywhere in its flat region and set the weight decay to the predicted product divided by that learning rate. The valley is long and narrow along the product direction, so fixing one coordinate and solving for the other loses nothing. The product falls like $D^{-0.6}$, so the weight memory in steps grows like $D^{0.6}$, slower than the run length. That is why the benefit of tuning weight decay shrinks with budget: at large budgets the default 0.1 is already close to optimal.

The expensive mistake is baseline (i). A weight decay of 1.6 that was right at 153.6M tokens is $17\times$ too large at 2.5B tokens and costs 0.084, more than any learning rate mistake in Problem~1.

\section{Batch-size scaling}
\subsection{What does the noisy quadratic model predict? (CPU)}

Setup: $f(w)=\tfrac12 w^\top Hw$ with $H=\mathrm{diag}(1,10)$, gradient noise covariance $\sigma^2 I$, $w_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\oplus\mathcal N(0,0.1)$, $N=8192$ samples per epoch so the number of updates is $N/B$, and 1024 Monte Carlo samples per loss estimate. The source batches are 1 to 64. We fit $\lrs\propto B^{p}$, predict $\lrs$ at $B=256$, and test the prediction with a sweep at 256.

\subsubsection*{(a)--(b) Scaling exponent by optimizer ($\sigma=1$)}

\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{p31_lr_vs_batch.png}
\caption{Optimal LR vs.\ batch for SGD, RMSProp, Adam ($\sigma=1$): source fits (dots, dashed), prediction at 256 ($\times$), measured optimum ($\square$).}
\label{fig:p31a}
\end{figure}

At low noise all three optimizers scale almost linearly with batch. The SGD rule breaks exactly where it should: the predicted 0.184 sits at the stability edge of the stiff direction ($\mathrm{lr}<2/10=0.2$), so the measured optimum backs off to 0.123. Adam and RMSProp have similar exponents, but Adam transfers better (gap 0.0005 versus 0.0031). Same exponent, different curve width, so a matching exponent does not guarantee matching transfer accuracy.

Tuned SGD loss by batch goes $0.00037$ ($B{=}1$) $\to$ $0.00032$ ($B{=}8$--16) $\to$ $0.00039$ ($B{=}64$) $\to$ $0.00073$ ($B{=}256$). The best batch is around 8 to 16. Past that the loss gets worse, because at fixed $N$ the number of updates is what you pay with and $B=256$ leaves only 32 of them. The rule holds while noise dominates the error. Once the budget is a handful of steps, the stability ceiling and the step count take over.

Prediction for the language model, made before P3.2: the optimal learning rate rises with batch, close to linearly from batch 8 to 64, then flattens by 128 to 256 where per step noise stops being the bottleneck.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering

\label{fig:p31c}

Signal to noise does change the rule. Adam's second moment is $v\approx g^2+\sigma^2/B$. When the gradient dominates, $v$ does not depend on batch, $g/\sqrt v$ behaves like SGD, and $\lrs\propto B$. When noise dominates, $\sqrt v\propto\sigma/\sqrt B$ already scales the update by $\sqrt B$, so the learning rate only needs to supply another $\sqrt B$: exponent $\tfrac12$. The 2-D and scalar models agree, so this comes from second moment normalisation, not from curvature. Curvature decides something else: where the linear rule breaks. The stiff direction imposes $\mathrm{lr}<2/\lambda_{\max}$ in 2-D, and the scalar model has no such ceiling.

Momentum helps a little at small batch: SGD goes from 0.00035 to 0.00032, and Adam with $\beta_1$ between 0.5 and 0.9 beats $\beta_1=0$ by 20\%. It hurts at large batch even after the learning rate is retuned: SGD with $\mu=0.9$ is $6\times$ worse, and Adam with $\beta_1\ge0.9$ is worse than 0.5. The tuned SGD learning rate drops $15\times$ with momentum at $B=256$ ($0.124\to0.0083$) because the effective step $\mathrm{lr}/(1-\mu)$ has to be cut to stop the stiff direction from overshooting.

Momentum averages gradients over about $1/(1-\beta_1)$ updates. That is noise reduction, which is also what a bigger batch does, so the benefit shrinks as the batch grows. The cost is lag. At $B=256$ a $\beta_1=0.9$ window of about 10 updates is a third of the 32 update run. Our expectation for the language model, written before P3.2(c): $\beta_1=0.9$ helps slightly at batch 8 and matters little at batch 256, because the language model run has thousands of updates rather than 32 and the lag cost should not appear. This turned out half right.

\item The learning rate optimum stopped rising. It grew like $B^{0.56}$ from 8 to 32, stayed flat from 32 to 64 ($0.0033\to0.0032$), was 0.0034 again at 128, and at 256 the best point is the \emph{lowest} learning rate we tried. Extrapolating the source law to 0.0056 and 0.0082 overshot, and the loss curve is steep on the high side: 0.012 at batch 256 costs 0.1.
\item At fixed weight decay 0.1, large batches simply lose. The best batch 256 point (3.080) is 0.16 worse than batch 32. With 2{,}344 updates instead of 18{,}750, a fixed per update decay means an $8\times$ longer memory in tokens, and the model carries early, badly fitted weights to the end.
\item The weight decay optimum doubled every time the batch doubled: best sampled 0.1 (16), 0.2 (32), 0.4 (64), 0.8 (128), 1.6 (256), with fitted target optima of 0.95 and 1.88. That is $\wds\propto B$. The proportional rule (0.95 / 1.91) was right to 2\%; the interior power law undershot because two of its four points were lower bounds. Scaling weight decay with batch keeps $\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd}\cdot(\text{updates per token})$, the decay timescale measured in tokens, constant. That is the same quantity Problem~2 found mattered.
\end{enumerate}

Even with the right weight decay, batch 256 is 0.05 worse than batch 32 at this token budget (2.971 vs.\ 2.919), and batch 128 is 0.015 worse. Fewer updates cannot be fully bought back with either knob. This agrees with the NQM only in the small batch range, where the exponent of about $\tfrac12$ matches its high noise regime. The NQM has no critical batch size and no weight decay, so it cannot predict the flattening or the $\mathrm{wd}\propto B$ rule.

\subsubsection*{(c) Momentum ablation}

We held each batch's best measured pair fixed (batch 8: lr 0.0015, wd 0.05; batch 256: lr 0.0015, wd 1.6), with $\beta_2=0.95$ and 614.4M tokens, and varied $\beta_1$ over $\{0,0.5,0.98\}$ plus the existing $\beta_1=0.9$ run.

\textbf{Momentum matters more at large batch, not less}, which is the opposite of the NQM's call. Dropping $\beta_1$ to 0 costs 0.04 at batch 8 and 0.23 at batch 256, five times as much. Raising it to 0.98 is free at batch 8 (2.934 vs.\ 2.935) but costs 0.054 at batch 256, so at large batch the optimum is pinned near 0.9 from both sides. One caveat: in P3.1(d) we retuned the learning rate for each $\beta_1$, but here it is held at the $\beta_1=0.9$ optimum, so the $\beta_1=0$ runs may be slightly undertuned. The gap of 0.23 is far beyond what a retune could recover given the shape of the batch 256 loss curve in P3.2(a).

Our reading, which is an interpretation rather than a measured mechanism: the NQM's ``momentum hurts at large batch'' result was lag in a 32 update run, where a $\beta_1=0.9$ window is a third of training. The language model at batch 256 has 2{,}344 updates, so that lag is negligible at 0.9 and only shows up at 0.98 (a window of about 50 updates, 2\% of the run, costing 0.054). What remains is the benefit. Adam with $\beta_1=0$ is RMSProp: every coordinate moves by about $\mathrm{lr}$ per update regardless of signal to noise. At batch 8 the 75{,}000 small steps under a long decaying schedule average that noise out on their own. At batch 256 each of the 2{,}344 steps is $32\times$ more consequential, and momentum is the only noise reduction left.

\subsubsection*{(d) What does the NQM explain?}

\begin{table}[H]\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.1cm} L L L}\toprule

$\lrs$ vs.\ batch, low noise & $\propto B^{0.9\text{--}1.0}$ & $\propto B^{0.56}$ for $8\to32$, then flat, then \emph{lower} at 128--256 ($0.003\to2.949$ beats $0.0056\to2.965$; $0.006\to3.080$ beats $0.0082\to3.136$) & \textbf{fails} above batch $\approx32$: the NQM has no critical batch size where more sequences per update stop reducing useful noise\\

best loss vs.\ batch & at fixed $N$: bigger batch better until the stability/step-count ceiling ($B\approx8$--16 best) & fixed tokens: 2.934 (8) $\to$ 2.919 (32) $\to$ 2.925 (64) $\to$ 2.934 (128, retuned wd) $\to$ 2.971 (256, retuned wd) & qualitatively agrees: an interior best batch, a penalty from the update count beyond it\\

\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

There are two currencies here: tokens, which you pay for, and updates, which move the weights. Doubling the batch halves the updates per token, and every hyperparameter defined per update has to be rescaled. The learning rate is per update, but once noise stops being the limit it is capped by curvature. That cap is a critical batch size, around 32 to 64 here. The NQM's low noise regime has no such thing because its noise never stops averaging down. Weight decay is also per update: $1/(\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd})$ updates of memory. Keeping it fixed while halving updates per token doubles the memory in tokens, so $\wds\propto B$ means ``keep the memory constant in tokens''. It is the Problem~2 product rule with batch as a third factor.

One change to the NQM would fix the biggest disagreement: add a per coordinate noise floor that does not shrink with batch (equivalently, a finite number of useful directions). Then gradient noise reduction saturates, the optimal step stops growing past a critical batch, and fixed token loss gets worse beyond it. Adding an $L_2$ term with a timescale in updates would also reproduce $\wds\propto B$. The practical rule for this model: when changing batch size at fixed tokens, keep the learning rate near its batch 32 to 64 value, scale weight decay in proportion to the batch, keep $\beta_1=0.9$, and expect a loss penalty at batch 128 and above anyway.

\section{Width and depth scaling}
\subsection*{4.0\quad Scaling rules from alignment assumptions}

Setup: width $n$, reference width $n_0$, $m=n/n_0$. Each matrix is used as $n^{-a}W$, initialised with i.i.d.\ entries of variance $n^{-2b}$, and updated by $\Delta W=-\eta\,n^{-c}U$ with $\lVert U\rVert_{\mathrm{RMS}}=\Theta(1)$. $a=0$ for hidden and input matrices, $c=0$ for the readout. The alignment ratios are $R(U,x_0)=\Theta(n^{\alpha})$, with $\alpha=1$ if the update direction is aligned with its input ($n$ terms add coherently) and $\alpha=\tfrac12$ if not (a random walk sum), and $S(V_0,\Delta x)=\Theta(n^{\omega})$, with $\omega=1$ aligned and $\tfrac12$ not. We assume $R(U,\Delta x)=O(n^{\alpha})$.

\paragraph{(a) Constraints.}
\emph{Hidden} ($a=0$, fan-in $k=n$). Order one features at initialisation: $\lVert Wx_0\rVert\sim\sqrt n\,n^{-b}=\Theta(1)\Rightarrow b=\tfrac12$ (variance $1/k$), independent of alignment. Order one direct update: $\lVert\Delta W x_0\rVert=\eta\,n^{-c}R(U,x_0)=\eta\,n^{\alpha-c}=\Theta(1)\Rightarrow c=\alpha$. Interaction: $\lVert\Delta W\,\Delta x\rVert=O(n^{\alpha-c})=O(1)$, bounded automatically.

\emph{Readout} ($c=0$, transposed $V_0\in\mathbb R^{n\times q}$, response $n^{-a}V^\top x$). Direct update: $\eta\,n^{\alpha-a}=\Theta(1)\Rightarrow a=\alpha$. Response of the initial readout to the feature change: $n^{-a}S(V_0,\Delta x)\,n^{-b}=n^{\omega-a-b}=\Theta(1)\Rightarrow b=\omega-\alpha$. Interaction $\eta\,n^{\alpha-a}=O(1)$, bounded.

\emph{Input / embedding} (fixed fan-in: a token hits one column, so there is no sum over $n$). Order one features $\Rightarrow b=0$ (variance 1). Order one update $\lVert\Delta W e_j\rVert=\eta\,n^{-c}\Rightarrow c=0$. Alignment plays no role.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}\toprule
alignment $(\alpha,\omega)$ & hidden $(a,b,c)$ & readout $(a,b,c)$ & input $(a,b,c)$\\\midrule
update aligned, readout aligned $(1,1)$ & $(0,\tfrac12,1)$ & $(1,0,0)$ & $(0,0,0)$\\
update aligned, readout not $(1,\tfrac12)$ & $(0,\tfrac12,1)$ & $(1,-\tfrac12,0)$ & $(0,0,0)$\\
update not aligned, readout aligned $(\tfrac12,1)$ & $(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ & $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$ & $(0,0,0)$\\
neither aligned $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ & $(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ & $(\tfrac12,0,0)$ & $(0,0,0)$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{(b) Rules in $m=n/n_0$.} Matching the table at $n_0$ gives a forward multiplier $m^{-a}$, an initialisation variance $\sigma_0^2\,m^{-2b}$, and a learning rate $\eta\,m^{-c}$.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}\toprule
layer & forward multiplier & init variance & learning rate\\\midrule
hidden & 1 & $1/k$ & $\eta\,m^{-\alpha}$\\
readout & $m^{-\alpha}$ & $n_0^{-1}\,m^{-2(\omega-\alpha)}$ & $\eta$\\
embedding & 1 & 1 & $\eta$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Update alignment $\alpha$ sets how fast hidden learning rates shrink ($1/m$ aligned, $1/\sqrt m$ not) and how strongly the readout is damped ($1/m$ vs.\ $1/\sqrt m$). Readout alignment $\omega$ only changes the readout initialisation. With $\omega=\alpha$ the variance does not depend on width ($1/n_0$). With $\omega<\alpha$ it would have to \emph{grow} with width ($m^{+1}$), which is the sign that ``aligned updates, unaligned readout'' is not a consistent regime. The interaction terms are $O(n^{\alpha-c})$ and $O(n^{\alpha-a})$, bounded in every row once the direct update constraint holds.

\muP{} is the case $(\alpha,\omega)=(1,1)$: hidden variance $1/k$ and learning rate $\eta/m$; readout variance $1/n_0$, multiplier $1/m$, learning rate $\eta$; embedding variance 1, learning rate $\eta$. This is exactly the P4.2 \muP{} column ($G/n_0$, $G/\sqrt{n_0}$, $1/m$, $\eta/m$ at $n_0=512$). Kaiming keeps the readout at $(0,\tfrac12,0)$ and hidden $c=0$. Its readout satisfies $a+b=\tfrac12=\omega$ only for an unaligned readout, and with aligned Adam updates its first step moves features by $\Theta(n)$ at fixed $\eta$, so its tuned base learning rate must fall like $1/n$. That is the prediction P4.1 tests.

That fits the fixed weight decay and decaying $\alpha$ mechanisms being largest when the width ratio is far from 1 in the shrinking direction. Once each is tuned, the two reach the same place (3.114 vs.\ 3.104).

A caveat: the baseline's 1024 curve is not smooth at the 0.02 level ($0.0014\to3.114$ vs.\ $0.0015\to3.135$, a gap present from step 300 on), so single runs at this width resolve learning rate differences of about 0.02 at best. The 0.128 and 0.067 headline gaps stand; the 0.007 \muP{} gap does not.

Gradient clipping (max norm 1) is active only at the start. The step 1 clip coefficient is 0.26 (baseline, width 128) and 0.11 (baseline, width 256) versus 0.034 and 0.040 under \muP, whose larger readout multiplier makes the initial gradient 3 to $8\times$ larger. It is identical across learning rates because the first gradient does not depend on the learning rate. After the first few dozen updates the coefficient is 1 at every logged point in every configuration, so clipping shapes the first steps and nothing after.

Update alignment decays during training. $\alpha\approx0.9$ at step 5, near the derivation's $\alpha=1$; 0.73 to 0.78 at step 100; 0.60 to 0.64 at the end, only a little above random ($\tfrac12$). Early training is a coherent, nearly rank one push; late training is closer to noise averaging. The $\eta/m$ rule is derived for $\alpha=1$, so it is most exact in the phase the five step test measured and increasingly over conservative later. That is consistent with \muP's optimum drifting \emph{up} at narrow widths over a full run.

Readout to feature change alignment never appears: $\omega_{\mathrm{move}}=0.50$ at every width, depth, prescription and step. So $\alpha=1$ holds early and fails late, and $\omega=1$ holds nowhere. The rules that depend on $\alpha$ still work approximately; the one that depends on $\omega$ is irrelevant to the learning rate.

Transients do not decide the final loss. \muP{} at width 128 starts with logits twice as large (1.96 vs.\ 0.98) and moves its features further in five steps (1.37 vs.\ 1.29), yet its loss at the transferred learning rate is \emph{better} (3.628 vs.\ 3.693). The baseline's features end up moving the most (2.02) and it is worst. The step size decided the outcome, not the size of the early transients.

Does a shifted optimum mean the model is outside the stable \muP{} regime? No. \muP's optimum moved $1.5\times$ between widths 512 and 128 while every stability signal stayed bounded: logit RMS grows the same way for all configurations, feature movement is 1.2 to 1.4, and the loss curves are smooth and flat. A drifting optimum with bounded diagnostics points to a mis-scaled \emph{secondary} quantity. Here that is the fixed weight decay (Problem~2's coupling: \muP{} multiplies the hidden learning rate by $1/m$ but leaves $\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd}$ unscaled, so the averaging window changes by $m$) together with the decaying $\alpha$, not a breakdown of feature learning.

Transferring a learning rate \emph{upward} in width works as-is with \muP{} (0.003 was the best point at 1024). With a standard parameterisation it only works through a fitted power law; direct transfer cost 0.128. After tuning, the two reach the same loss to within 0.01 to 0.02, so over a real budget \muP{} buys predictability rather than performance. That is the opposite emphasis from the five step test, where it bought both. Depth corrections are irrelevant at depths 4 to 16. And whenever the width ratio exceeds about $2\times$, weight decay needs another look: the $\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd}$ timescale is the one quantity none of these prescriptions preserves, and it is the most likely cause of \muP's remaining optimum drift at narrow widths.
