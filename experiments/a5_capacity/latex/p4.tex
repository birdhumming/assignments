\section*{Problem 4: Looping}

All looped models are d8w512 with one prelude block, one coda block and a core of $R$ applications of $B$ distinct blocks (\texttt{p4\_looping.py:LoopLM}); the dense d8 is the same code with $B=6$ distinct core blocks applied once each. Table~\ref{tab:p4ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:p4ladder} give the ladders; Table~\ref{tab:p4boundary} the boundary maps; Figure~\ref{fig:p4eval} the inference-time pass sweep; Table~\ref{tab:p4switch} the mid-training switches.

\textbf{(a) Fewer distinct blocks at fixed compute.} Holding eight core applications fixed, every halving of the number of distinct blocks costs loss, and the cost is roughly constant per halving: 6 blocks 2.927, 3 blocks 2.980 ($+0.053$), 2 blocks 3.007 ($+0.027$ more), 1 block 3.048 ($+0.041$ more). The one-block loop is 0.12 nats behind the dense model at identical FLOPs, which on the Problem 1 yardstick is the gap between d8 and d5 ($\epsilon\approx0.45$). So at this scale the loop is a parameter-saving device, not a compute-saving one: the curves in Figure~\ref{fig:p4ladder} (left) are parallel from update 2{,}000 on, there is no late crossover, and the ranking is the parameter ranking.

\textbf{(b) Does more looping help at fixed parameters?} The one-block ladder holds the parameter count fixed at 15.6M (prelude, one core block, coda) and varies how often the core is applied. Two passes give 3.086, three 3.066, four 3.066, six 3.048 (the loop-r6 model, whose core is the same single block), twelve 3.048 and eighteen 3.064 (the four- and twelve-pass values are checkpoint NLLs on the diagnostic set, marked $^\dagger$ in the table; the nine-pass run lost both its W\&B log and its checkpoint to a run-id collision and is reported as missing), so the gain saturates: each extra application up to six buys about 0.01 nats, and doubling or tripling the passes beyond that buys nothing while costing 1.7--2.5$\times$ the FLOPs of the dense model. The 18-pass model is also the most brittle at other pass counts (3.93 at 6 passes, 3.12 at 24). Compared with the fixed-application ladder, re-applying one block six times (3.048) recovers 0.07 of the 0.12-nat gap between the 15.6M one-block model at two passes and the 34.6M dense model: looping converts compute into quality, but at a worse rate than adding fresh parameters (the dense model spends the same 8 applications and is 0.12 better than the six-pass loop). %P4B%

\textbf{(c) Boundary maps.} The six-pass one-block loop improves from 3.048 to 3.005 when the core input is RMS-normalized at each pass boundary and the prelude output is re-injected (``recall'', $h \leftarrow \text{norm}(h) + e$). That recovers 0.044 of the 0.12 gap to dense and puts the one-block model level with the two-block loop (3.007) at a third of the core parameters. Boundary normalization \emph{alone} fails outright: the run falls to the unigram loss (6.87) by update 40 and never recovers. Its signal profile shows why: after the first RMS-normalized boundary the next block's output has RMS 246 (vs 5--7 in the plain loop), the stream saturates at RMS 321 for the remaining passes, and the gradient reaching the first two applications is exactly zero. Normalizing the residual stream to unit scale between passes while the blocks keep adding updates of RMS 5--10 makes each pass overwrite the previous one; re-injecting the prelude output (recall) restores a persistent reference signal, and with it the stream stays at RMS 5.6--8.2 across all eight applications with gradients within a factor of 20 of each other, against a monotone growth from 5.3 to 24.3 in the plain loop. In short, the norm fixes the \emph{scale} drift of the loop but destroys the \emph{content}; recall supplies the content.

\textbf{(d) Deep one-block loops and evaluating at unseen pass counts.} Figure~\ref{fig:p4eval} evaluates each trained loop at pass counts it never saw. The plain loops are brittle: loop-r6 (trained at 6) is 3.06 at 6 passes, 3.11 at 8, 3.35 at 12 and 4.21 at 24, and the two- and three-pass loops degrade even faster (loop-r2: 3.00 at 2, 3.44 at 6, 5.01 at 24). Running fewer passes than trained is worse still (loop-r6 at 1 pass: 4.46). With boundary norm and recall the picture changes completely: 3.02 at the trained 6 passes, 3.03 at 7--24 passes, flat to three decimals. The normalized, re-anchored loop has learned a contraction: extra applications leave the stream essentially where it was, so the model can be run deeper for free (and shallower at a modest cost: 3.07 at 4 passes, 3.10 at 3). The one-block two-pass model behaves like the plain loops (3.10 at 2 passes, 3.16 at 3, 4.09 at 8, 5.91 at 24). Recall alone (prelude output re-added at every boundary, no norm) is also worth having: 3.022 vs 3.048 for the plain six-pass loop, with norm+recall at 3.005. %P4D%

\textbf{(e) Changing sharing mid-training.} Table~\ref{tab:p4switch} switches the 6-pass core at update 4,688 (half of training), with the two Adam-reset controls trained from the same seed. Going from unique blocks to a tied block is destructive: the run was at 3.114 just before the switch, jumped to 5.23 at the first evaluation afterwards (the six blocks are averaged into one, which is nowhere near any of them) and only recovered to 3.104 by the end, worse than the 3.048 of a loop that was tied throughout. Resetting Adam at the same step without changing the architecture costs nothing for the tied loop (3.0486 vs 3.0484), so the damage comes from the weight merge, not from the optimizer state. The opposite direction is benign: untying the block (six copies of the shared weights, which continue from exactly the same function) reaches a held-out NLL of 2.991 on the diagnostic set, between the tied loop (3.034 on the same set) and the always-unique dense model (2.935); the W\&B log of that run was lost, so only the checkpoint diagnostic is available, and the unique-weights Adam-reset control (3.002) shows that a fresh optimizer on 8 unique blocks mid-schedule costs about 0.07 by itself, which is most of the gap to the dense model. So a tied warm start followed by untying recovers most of the dense model's loss at half the parameter count for the first half of training, while late tying throws away what the unique blocks had learned.
